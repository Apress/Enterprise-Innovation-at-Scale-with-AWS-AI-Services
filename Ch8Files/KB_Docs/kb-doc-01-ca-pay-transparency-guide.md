# California Pay Transparency Compliance Guide

## Document Purpose
This guide synthesizes California Senate Bill 1162 (SB 1162) and related pay transparency requirements into actionable guidance for HR professionals posting job descriptions. Current as of January 2024.

---

## Part 1: SB 1162 Overview

### What is SB 1162?
California Senate Bill 1162 (Labor Code Section 244) requires private employers to disclose pay range information in job postings. This law aims to reduce pay inequality and provide candidates with transparency about compensation expectations.

### Effective Date
**January 1, 2024**

SB 1162 applies to all job postings made on or after this date. If you posted something before 2024, you're not required to retroactively update it. But all new postings must comply.

### Who Must Comply
- All private employers with employees in California
- Employers posting positions available to California residents
- Applies even if the job is not physically located in California
- Includes remote positions available to CA applicants

### What Triggers the Requirement
The requirement applies when an employer:
- Advertises a position
- Makes the position available for application
- Post on a job board, company website, or directly to candidates

If you post on LinkedIn, Indeed, Glassdoor, or any job board, you must include the pay range.

## Part 2: What Must Be Disclosed

### The Pay Range Requirement

**You must disclose:** A pay range for any job posting.

**Format:** The range should include:
- **Minimum compensation**: The lowest amount you'd pay for the position
- **Maximum compensation**: The highest amount you'd pay for the position

**Example (CORRECT):**
```
Salary: $85,000 - $120,000 per year
```

**Example (CORRECT):**
```
Compensation: $4,000 - $6,500 per month
```

**Example (INCORRECT - Too Vague):**
```
Competitive salary
```

**Example (INCORRECT - No Range):**
```
$95,000 annually
```

### What "Compensation" Includes

Per SB 1162, compensation includes:
- Base salary or wages
- Commission
- Bonus (if typically offered)
- Stock options and equity
- Benefits with monetary value (health insurance, retirement match, etc.) that are standard for the role

### What You Can Exclude

You do not need to include:
- Discretionary bonuses or incentives
- Benefits that vary by employee choice
- Flexible spending accounts
- Shift differentials (unless standard for the role)

### Exceptions to the Requirement

SB 1162 provides limited exceptions. You do NOT need to disclose pay range if:

1. **The position is physically located outside California**
   - BUT if applicants from CA can apply, you still must disclose

2. **The position is being filled from an internal promotion pool only**
   - Not accepting external applications
   - Must still disclose if you later open it externally

3. **You are a covered employer and made a good faith effort to determine range**
   - And could not ascertain it in good faith
   - Documentation required; rarely accepted

### Regional Pay Bands

If your position covers multiple regions or states, provide:
- **Either:** One range covering the highest and lowest pay
- **Or:** Region-specific ranges if compensation varies significantly

**Example (Multi-Region):**
```
Salary Range:
- San Francisco/Bay Area: $110,000 - $150,000
- Los Angeles: $95,000 - $130,000
- Remote (anywhere in CA): $85,000 - $125,000
```

## Part 3: Common Compliance Issues

### Issue 1: Missing Pay Range Entirely

**Problem:** Posting says "Competitive compensation" or "DOE" (Depends on Experience) without numbers.

**Why It Violates SB 1162:** The law requires specific numbers - a minimum and maximum.

**How to Fix:** Add explicit range: "Salary: $70,000 - $95,000 per year, depending on experience"

### Issue 2: Range Too Broad

**Problem:** "$50,000 - $200,000" - spread is so wide it's meaningless.

**Why It's Risky:** While technically compliant, an excessively broad range may invite regulatory scrutiny. California labor board guidance suggests ranges within 50% of the minimum are more credible.

**How to Fix:** Narrow the range to reflect realistic pay progression: "$85,000 - $125,000" (47% spread)

### Issue 3: Using Vague Terms

**Problem:** "Salary negotiable," "Competitive based on experience," "Based on qualifications"

**Why It Violates SB 1162:** Doesn't provide the actual range.

**How to Fix:** Replace with numbers: "Salary: $75,000 - $100,000, adjusted based on experience and qualifications"

### Issue 4: Hiding Range in Benefits Description

**Problem:** "We offer a competitive package including $50,000-$70,000 salary + benefits"

**Why It May Not Comply:** The range must be clearly stated upfront in the main job description or summary, not buried in benefits section.

**How to Fix:** Put pay range in a dedicated line at the top or in a compensation section.

### Issue 5: Remote and In-Office Pay Variation

**Problem:** Posting says one salary for remote, different for office, but doesn't specify which.

**Why It's Unclear:** Candidates don't know which they'll get.

**How to Fix:** Explicitly specify: "Salary: $80,000-$110,000 (office at HQ), $75,000-$105,000 (remote)"

## Part 4: Age and Discrimination Considerations

### Don't Use Years of Experience to Proxy for Pay

**Problem Example:**
```
"Senior Software Engineer with 15+ years required. Salary: $120,000 - $160,000"
```

**Risk:** The combination of years requirement + high salary may indicate age discrimination.

**Better Approach:**
```
"Software Engineer with significant enterprise experience.
Salary range: $100,000 - $140,000 depending on skills and background."
```

Focusing on skills rather than years reduces discrimination risk.

### Gender-Neutral Language

Make sure your pay disclosure uses gender-neutral framing.

**Problem:** "We pay our salesmen $50,000-$70,000"

**Better:** "Sales professionals earn $50,000-$70,000"

## Part 5: Implementation Best Practices

### Best Practice 1: Determine Your Range First

Before posting, decide:
1. What's the minimum you'd pay a qualified candidate?
2. What's the maximum for this role?
3. Is this based on actual budget, comparable positions, market data?

Document your reasoning. If challenged, having a business justification helps.

### Best Practice 2: Be Honest

Set ranges you can actually pay. If you list $80,000-$120,000 but really never go above $95,000, candidates will feel misled when they negotiate.

### Best Practice 3: Update as Needed

If you repost a position or if market rates change, you can adjust your posted range. Just make sure new posts include updated ranges.

### Best Practice 4: Document Your Method

Keep records of:
- Market research you conducted
- How you determined the range
- Any adjustments made for regional differences
- Dates the ranges were set

If audited, this documentation demonstrates good faith compliance.

### Best Practice 5: Train Hiring Managers

Make sure hiring managers know:
- The range must be posted (they can't negotiate below it)
- They should hire within the posted range
- Outlier negotiations should be documented

## Part 6: Enforcement and Penalties

### Who Enforces?
- California Division of Labor Standards Enforcement (DLSE)
- California Attorney General
- Private right of action (employees can sue directly)

### Penalties for Violation

**Civil Penalty:** $100 to $10,000 per violation (per violation = per job posting)

**Private Lawsuits:** Employees may file complaints. If found liable, employers may owe:
- Attorney's fees
- Costs
- Damages
- In some cases, injunctive relief

**Example Penalty Calculation:**
- Company posted 10 non-compliant job postings
- Minimum penalty: 10 × $100 = $1,000
- Maximum penalty: 10 × $10,000 = $100,000
- Plus attorney fees could be $5,000-$50,000+

### Investigation Process

If DLSE investigates:
1. They'll request job postings from a specified period
2. They'll check for pay range disclosure
3. They'll assess whether ranges are reasonable
4. Employer gets opportunity to respond

## Part 7: Interaction with Other Laws

### SB 1162 + Equal Pay Laws

California Equal Pay Act requires equal pay for substantially similar work. SB 1162 transparency about ranges supports this law because:
- If ranges are transparent, pay gaps are visible
- This creates pressure to pay fairly
- Helps identify discriminatory pay practices

### SB 1162 + Salary History Ban

California banned asking for salary history. This means:
- You can't base offers on prior pay
- Must set ranges based on market and job requirements
- This makes the pay range even more important

## Part 8: Compliance Checklist for Job Postings

Use this checklist when posting any California position:

- [ ] Position is for California (or open to California applicants)
- [ ] Pay range is explicitly stated (minimum and maximum)
- [ ] Range is in numbers, not vague terms like "competitive"
- [ ] Range is prominent (not hidden in fine print)
- [ ] If multiple regions, ranges specified for each
- [ ] Range is realistic and defensible
- [ ] No age-proxy language (years of experience tied to pay)
- [ ] No gender-biased pay language
- [ ] Hiring manager trained on range
- [ ] Records kept of how range was determined
- [ ] Posting will be removed or updated when position closes

## Part 9: FAQ

**Q: Do I have to pay everyone at the posted minimum?**
A: No. The range is what you're willing to pay. You can hire someone at $85,000 if the range is $85,000-$120,000.

**Q: Can I post a range that's lower than I actually plan to pay?**
A: Technically the law doesn't prohibit it, but it's bad practice. Candidates will be upset if offered outside the posted range.

**Q: What if the job changes mid-recruitment?**
A: If requirements change, you should update the posting with a new range. Don't post one range and pay another.

**Q: Does SB 1162 apply to contractors?**
A: Yes, if you're posting positions for contractor roles available to California residents.

**Q: What about positions advertised in other states - do I need to disclose in CA?**
A: Only if the position is available to CA residents. If it's CA-specific, yes. If it's out of state only, no.

## Summary

SB 1162 requires transparent pay disclosure in job postings. This is straightforward: include a minimum and maximum compensation figure. The law supports pay equity, candidate transparency, and fair hiring practices.

**Key Takeaway:** Always include a specific pay range when posting in California or to California residents. It's required by law, reduces litigation risk, and supports fair hiring practices.
